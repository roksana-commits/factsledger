## Overview

The Cambodia Daily was an independent, English-language daily newspaper published in Phnom Penh from 1993 to 2017, and one of its own investigative pieces — a 2002 profile weighing trafficking allegations against adoption facilitator [Lauryn Galindo](/people/lauryn-galindo/) — is today one of the most detailed independent accounts of her Cambodia-based adoption operation. The paper was founded by Bernard Krisher, a former Newsweek correspondent, with the explicit aim of building independent journalism in Cambodia after the Khmer Rouge era, and it operated under the motto "All the News Without Fear or Favor" ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/cambodia-daily-free-press.php)). It closed abruptly in September 2017 after the Cambodian government issued it a disputed $6.3 million tax bill, an episode widely described by press-freedom monitors as part of a broader pre-election crackdown ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/parallels/2017/09/03/548296895/cambodia-daily-newspaper-to-close-after-24-years)).

## Investigative reporting

Over 24 years, The Cambodia Daily built a reputation for investigative reporting on illegal logging, land grabs, corruption and human rights abuses, training generations of Cambodian journalists in the process; several of its alumni went on to major international outlets including The New York Times ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/cambodia-daily-free-press.php)). A former executive editor, writing in TIME, recalled the newsroom's investigations into political killings, land grabs by officials' families, human trafficking, and a bribery scheme involving an Australian mining company, as well as the paper's 2012 reporting that helped unravel the public account of anti-trafficking advocate Somaly Mam ([TIME](https://time.com/4945666/cambodia-daily-newspaper-memoir-hun-sen/)). Among its investigative output was a June 22, 2002 profile of Lauryn Galindo, then a prominent Cambodia-based adoption facilitator, which examined both trafficking allegations against her and her charitable claims — the same article FactsLedger's own profile of Galindo relies on for much of her Cambodia-era background ([The Cambodia Daily](https://english.cambodiadaily.com/2002/06/22/is-lauryn-galindo-a-baby-buyer-a-benefactor-or-both/)).

## The 2017 tax dispute and closure

In August 2017, Cambodia's General Department of Taxation issued the paper a $6.3 million back-tax bill and gave it roughly a month to pay before ceasing operations; the paper's editor said she had requested a formal audit but never received one, and that the sum was impossible to pay ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/parallels/2017/09/03/548296895/cambodia-daily-newspaper-to-close-after-24-years)). Prime Minister Hun Sen publicly called the paper's leadership "thieves" and told them to "pack up and go," and the closure came alongside a broader crackdown that included tax notices against Radio Free Asia's and Voice of America's Khmer-language services and the shuttering of opposition-aligned radio stations ([Global Voices Advox](https://advox.globalvoices.org/2017/09/06/cambodia-daily-closes-down-after-government-threatened-it-with-hefty-tax-bill/)). The Cambodia Daily published its final Cambodia edition on September 4, 2017, under the headline "Descent Into Outright Dictatorship," reporting the arrest of opposition leader Kem Sokha on treason charges on the same front page ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/cambodia-daily-free-press.php)). The organization's own account states that "government pressure forced us to leave Phnom Penh" that year; it has since operated as a nonprofit, digital-only news service based in Washington, D.C., working with reporters still inside Cambodia ([The Cambodia Daily](https://english.cambodiadaily.com/about-us/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not resolve conflicting circulation figures reported for the paper at closure (roughly 4,000 daily per the Columbia Journalism Review versus 5,000-8,000 six days a week per the Media Ownership Monitor), nor a one-day discrepancy between the Columbia Journalism Review's account of when the government's tax order was issued and NPR's account of the same events. It does not independently verify the paper's exact first-issue date beyond the founding year, 1993, and treats the reported "58 staff layoffs" figure as coming from a single outlet rather than as independently corroborated. It does not characterize the substance of the 2002 Galindo profile beyond noting that the paper published it; that article's specific factual claims are addressed and separately sourced in FactsLedger's own profile of Lauryn Galindo, not here.
