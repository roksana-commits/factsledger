## Overview

The National Pro Bono Centre (NPBC) is a UK charity and company limited by guarantee that coordinates free legal-help charities operating in England and Wales. It was jointly established by three existing national pro bono bodies — the [Bar Pro Bono Unit](/organizations/advocate-charity/) (now known as Advocate), [LawWorks](/organizations/lawworks/) (the Solicitors Pro Bono Group) and the ILEX Pro Bono Forum — and formally opened on 19 October 2010 at 48 Chancery Lane in London's legal district ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/first-ever-national-legal-pro-bono-centre-launched)). An earlier Counsel report from 30 June 2010 previewed the planned summer opening at the same Chancery Lane address, describing it as a shared home for the three organisations' "clearing houses" ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/new-headquarters-pro-bono)). Companies House records the underlying company as incorporated on 16 April 2010 ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/07226359)).

## Founding and purpose

Covering the launch, Counsel magazine quoted the Law Society's then chief executive describing the Centre as "unique in this country and...a world first," on the basis that it brought together, for the first time, coordinating bodies from all three branches of the legal profession — barristers, solicitors and legal executives — working in the public interest under one roof ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/first-ever-national-legal-pro-bono-centre-launched)). The stated aim was "a more joined-up service to members of the public and to community groups in need of legal help" who could not access legal aid or afford private representation.

## Structure and current activities

The Centre is a registered charity, number 1137708, according to the Charity Commission's own register, which also records its income for 2022 at £142,780, with seven trustees and eight volunteers ([The Charity Commission](https://register-of-charities.charitycommission.gov.uk/charity-search/-/charity-details/5010184)). By the Centre's own account, its original Chancery Lane office lease expired during the pandemic in 2020, after which it moved to supporting pro bono initiatives across the sector remotely rather than from a single fixed office ([National Pro Bono Centre](https://www.nationalprobonocentre.org.uk/about-us/)). It now describes its base as the Justice Hub, a shared workspace at the International Dispute Resolution Centre on Paternoster Lane in London, which it says it co-founded with the charities JUSTICE and Advocate as a collaborative space for organisations working on access to justice ([National Pro Bono Centre](https://www.nationalprobonocentre.org.uk/what-we-do/thejusticehub/)). Its current Chief Executive is named on its own website as Esther McConnell ([National Pro Bono Centre](https://www.nationalprobonocentre.org.uk/about-us/our-team/)).

## Relationship to the Free Representation Unit

The Centre publishes case studies documenting the work of pro bono charities in England and Wales, including one describing a Free Representation Unit volunteer's tribunal win while working at [FRU's partner Legal Advice Centre at Nottingham Law School](/organizations/nls-legal/), now branded NLS Legal ([National Pro Bono Centre](https://www.nationalprobonocentre.org.uk/casestudy/anitas-story/)). That same case study is cited independently, as a description of FRU's volunteer model in practice, in FactsLedger's own profile of the [Free Representation Unit](/organizations/free-representation-unit/).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile verifies the Centre's 2010 founding, launch date and founding organisations against two Counsel magazine reports from that year, and its charity number, 2022 income and governance figures against the Charity Commission's own register, but it does not independently verify the Centre's current (2026) finances, staffing or full activity beyond what its own website states. The claim that its Chancery Lane office closed in 2020 because the pandemic-era lease expired, and the date the Justice Hub itself was established, rest on the Centre's own account and were not corroborated in an independent source. It does not attempt a full history of the Centre's activities between its 2010 founding and the present.
