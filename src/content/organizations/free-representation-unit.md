## Overview

The Free Representation Unit (FRU) is a London-based UK charity founded in 1972 by law students at bar school with the aim of relieving the impact of poverty by providing legal representation in tribunals to people who could not afford lawyers ([The Bar Council](https://www.barcouncil.org.uk/resource/free-legal-representation-justice-week-2020.html)). It represents clients in three main areas: employment tribunals (including the Employment Appeal Tribunal), benefit appeals in the first-tier and Upper Tribunals, and criminal injuries compensation cases — areas where legal aid is largely unavailable despite involving complex statute and case law, and where outcomes, such as disability benefit entitlements reported to be worth over £8,000 per year, can be life-changing for claimants. Writing in Counsel, the official magazine of the Bar of England and Wales, in 2011, ahead of FRU's 40th anniversary, the publication described it as "the largest single provider of free advocacy in the UK" ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/pro-bono)).

## Founding

Counsel's 2011 history of pro bono representation credits FRU's creation to David Guy, "then a student member of Gray's Inn and now a barrister at Tanfield Chambers," and reports that the group initially organised under the name "Bar Students for Legal Advice," before it "had acquired the name FRU" by December 1972, by which point it had already taken on around 30 cases ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/pro-bono)). This is consistent with, and adds a named individual to, the Bar Council's own account that the unit was founded by a group of law students at bar school.

## How it works

FRU's casework is carried out by trained volunteer representatives — mainly law students and early-career lawyers — supervised by paid legal officers, primarily across London and the South East, with cases also referred through registered agencies or, for shorter employment matters, directly from the public via a Self Referral Scheme. A partner Legal Advice Centre at Nottingham Law School — now branded [NLS Legal](/organizations/nls-legal/) — extends this model into the Midlands: in one documented case, a Nottingham Law School student volunteering with FRU successfully challenged an employment tribunal's incorrectly imposed stay of proceedings on behalf of a client, after which the tribunal awarded the client more than £25,000 for unfair dismissal and breach of contract, according to a case study published by the [National Pro Bono Centre](/organizations/national-pro-bono-centre/) ([National Pro Bono Centre](https://www.nationalprobonocentre.org.uk/casestudy/anitas-story/)).

## Scope and recognition

Beyond representing individual clients, the FRU gives volunteer law students genuine courtroom experience before they qualify and a practical understanding of how the law affects ordinary people's lives. The Bar Council's own account notes a persistent gap between need and capacity: the unit "can only represent hundreds of people each year when there are tens of thousands of people who need help," describing "advice deserts" where no affordable legal services exist. The FRU marked its 50th anniversary in 2022, and is represented on the Attorney General for England and Wales's Pro Bono Committee. In a 2007 interview later republished by the legal careers publication LawCareers.Net, then chief executive Clive Tulloch pointed to the courtroom experience FRU gives volunteers as being different from anything taught at university, adding: "Our alumni include Baroness Scotland, Cherie Blair and Tony Blair!" ([LawCareers.Net](https://lawcareers.net/Information/Features/30102007-The-Free-Representation-Unit-FRU-the-Looking-Glass)). By the time Counsel profiled the unit in 2011, FRU volunteers were reported to have represented 1,168 clients the previous year in social security, employment and criminal injury cases, and the organisation had launched a pilot project in Nottingham — its first presence outside London ([Counsel](https://www.counselmagazine.co.uk/articles/pro-bono)), a forerunner of the partner Legal Advice Centre FRU maintains with Nottingham Law School today.

## Funding and staffing

FRU's own annual report and financial statements for the 2023 financial year record total income of £496,710 against total expenditure of £505,139, leaving reserves of £162,887, with funding drawn from bodies including the Bar Council (£67,400), the Inns of Court (£80,000), Linklaters LLP (£50,000), the City Bridge Trust (£19,750), Trust for London (£24,500), the Employment Lawyers Association (£20,000) and the London Legal Support Trust (£29,615) ([Free Representation Unit](https://www.thefru.org.uk/sites/default/files/Annual%20Report%202023.pdf)). Linklaters' support is longstanding: profiling FRU in 2021, the trade publication The Lawyer described the firm as "one of FRU's most important and longstanding supporters," seconding a trainee solicitor to FRU for a six-month placement every year, alongside a small core staff of legal officers who worked alongside FRU's volunteers ([The Lawyer](https://www.thelawyer.com/the-free-representation-unit-powers-on/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile verifies FRU's FY2023 finances and major funders against FRU's own audited annual report, and verifies the 2011 caseload snapshot, founder name and "largest single provider" description against Counsel magazine, but it does not independently verify FRU's current (2026) annual caseload, staffing or funding — the most recent figures here are from 2023 (finances) and 2021 (staffing). The claim that Baroness Scotland, Cherie Blair and Tony Blair are FRU alumni rests on a single on-the-record statement by a former FRU chief executive, reported by one specialist legal careers publication; it was not independently corroborated against a second source and should be read as attributed to that interview rather than separately confirmed. It does not attempt a full history of the organization between its 1972 founding and its 50th anniversary in 2022.
