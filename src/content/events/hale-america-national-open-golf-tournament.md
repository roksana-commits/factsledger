## Overview

The Hale America National Open Golf Tournament was organized as a wartime substitute for the 1942 U.S.
Open after the United States Golf Association suspended its national championships following the attack on
Pearl Harbor, when the scheduled host, Interlachen Country Club in Minnesota, also withdrew
([Global Golf Post](https://www.globalgolfpost.com/featured/ben-hogan-hale-america-and-the-myth-of-the-fifth-open-title-2/)).
Tom McMahon of the Chicago District Golf Association conceived it as a war-relief fundraiser, jointly run by
the USGA, the PGA of America and the CDGA, with proceeds benefiting the Navy Relief Society and the USO
([Texas Sports Hall of Fame](https://www.tshof.org/news/did-ben-hogan-win-5-us-opens)). Held 18–21 June 1942
at Ridgemoor Country Club in Chicago, the event modified the usual U.S. Open format — raising the amateur
handicap limit, restructuring qualifying into 69 local and 14 sectional sites, and capping the final field
at roughly 100 players, with exemptions for veterans such as Bobby Jones, Walter Hagen and Jock Hutchison.

## Result

Ben Hogan won by three strokes with a score of 271, 17 under par, including a second-round 62 — a margin
that, had it counted, would have broken the U.S. Open scoring record by ten strokes. Jimmy Demaret and Mike
Turnesa finished tied for second. A Detroit Free Press report the following day carried the headline "Hogan
Wins Hale Open with 17-Under-Par 271"
([RareNewspapers.com, Detroit Free Press, 22 June 1942](https://www.rarenewspapers.com/view/672120)).

## The "fifth Open" dispute

The USGA was explicit at the time that the tournament would not be treated as an official championship.
Executive Secretary Joe Dey stated that "the tournament will not be a championship, as the U.S.G.A. has
decided its usual national championships should not properly be held in wartime." The medal Hogan received
carried no "Open Championship" designation. Contemporary opposition to counting the result was vocal: 1941
U.S. Open champion Craig Wood called the tournament "farcical," pointing to Ridgemoor's easy scoring
conditions, and Bobby Jones argued that, with players such as Sam Snead absent, "it wouldn't be fair to call
it a championship tournament and put the results in the record book." Year-end reviews by sportswriters in
December 1942 treated Snead's PGA Championship win as the year's only major
([Global Golf Post](https://www.globalgolfpost.com/featured/ben-hogan-hale-america-and-the-myth-of-the-fifth-open-title-2/)).
Gene Sarazen mounted a public campaign for the USGA to recognize the result; it did not succeed, and the
USGA has never added Hale America to the U.S. Open's official record.

Hogan privately regarded the win as his own first U.S. Open title. According to a widely told story, when
congratulated on his official 1950 U.S. Open win at Merion, he replied simply: "Third"
([Texas Sports Hall of Fame](https://www.tshof.org/news/did-ben-hogan-win-5-us-opens)). He generally stopped
pressing the claim publicly for roughly a decade afterward, though he occasionally reasserted it later in
life. This profile does not take a position on whether the result should count as a major championship — it
reports the tournament's own contemporaneous status and the arguments made on both sides since.

## What this profile does not independently confirm

One source describes a 1990 USGA museum exhibit that displayed Hogan with five U.S. Open medals, which would
implicitly include this tournament — a detail reported here as that source describes it, not independently
verified against the exhibit itself. A cited fundraising total of roughly $25,000 likewise comes from a
single source.
