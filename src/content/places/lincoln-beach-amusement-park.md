## Overview

Lincoln Beach was a segregation-era beach and amusement park in New Orleans East, built as a recreational
space for the city's Black residents after a 1934 seawall built to protect white neighborhoods made the
Black-frequented Seabrook Beach dangerous, leading the city to ban swimming there in 1943. In 1938, fruit
importer Samuel Zemurray donated an unproductive orchard across the Industrial Canal to create an
alternative — though the site was initially remote, lacked public transit access, and was contaminated by
nearby sewage-dumping fishing camps, prompting health officials to recommend its closure
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).

## Redevelopment and its years as a destination

Between 1951 and 1954, the Orleans Parish Levee Board invested in transforming the site, building a new
bathhouse, a ten-acre parking lot, three pools and a restaurant, with private operators adding amusement
rides. Its May 1954 dedication drew thousands of visitors and featured the marching band from Booker T.
Washington High School. Over the following decade, Lincoln Beach became a major recreational destination
for Black New Orleanians, hosting performers including Fats Domino, Irma Thomas and Nat King Cole
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).

## Closure and legacy

The park closed abruptly in 1964: after the Civil Rights Act's passage, and shortly after the previously
whites-only Pontchartrain Beach was integrated, the white-owned operator invoked an escape clause in its
agreement and shut the site down immediately, leaving rides including the Ferris wheel to rust in place
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).
In 2024, the National Park Service added Lincoln Beach to the National Register of Historic Places, a
listing city officials said would "unlock more funding options" for a planned $24.6 million redevelopment
that includes a fishing pier, an expanded swimming beach, a lakeside walkway, trails and an elevated
pedestrian bridge over Hayne Boulevard — reported at the time to be on track for a summer 2025 reopening as
the city's first public beach in decades
([Axios New Orleans](https://www.axios.com/local/new-orleans/2024/07/24/lincoln-beach-reopen-2025-national-register-historic-places)).
