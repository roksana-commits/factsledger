## Overview

Arthur Gaeth (6 May 1905 – 15 April 1984) was born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and was baptized into the
Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints at age eight in 1913
([Church History Biographical Database](https://history.churchofjesuschrist.org/chd/individual/arthur-gaeth-1905?lang=eng)).
He went on to lead the first Latter-day Saint mission established in a Slavic country before later
becoming a radio journalist who reported live from one of the twentieth century's most consequential
trials. Set apart on 11 May 1926 by Church president David O. McKay, he served as a missionary in the
German-Austrian Mission's Berlin and Vienna Conferences until his release in May 1929
([Church History Biographical Database](https://history.churchofjesuschrist.org/chd/individual/arthur-gaeth-1905?lang=eng)).
Weeks later, on 24 July 1929, European Mission president John A. Widtsoe dedicated the land for
missionary work in Czechoslovakia and called the 24-year-old Gaeth to preside over the new Czechoslovak
Mission — the first Latter-day Saint mission in a Slavic country
([Ensign Peak Foundation](https://ensignpeakfoundation.org/czechoslovak-mission-monument/)); the church's
own mission-level records independently corroborate the same founding date and his tenure through 11 June
1936
([Church History Biographical Database](https://history.churchofjesuschrist.org/chd/organization/mission/czechoslovak-1929?lang=eng)).

## Building the Czechoslovak Mission

According to a Brigham Young University Religious Studies Center account, Gaeth's mother was a Czech
national who had years earlier received a patriarchal blessing promising that he would carry the gospel
to her homeland; he was selected in June 1929 to lead the new mission together with five other
missionaries under his direction
([Religious Studies Center, BYU](https://rsc.byu.edu/mission-president-spy/immersing-himself-bohemian-life)).
He and his future wife, Martha Kralickova, purchased a villa in Prague that served as the mission's
headquarters. As mission president, Gaeth ran an active public-relations campaign: he gave radio
broadcasts on 3 and 7 August 1929 announcing the church's arrival, scheduled lectures at German
educational institutions, and contributed newspaper articles about the church's presence in the country
([Religious Studies Center, BYU](https://rsc.byu.edu/mission-president-spy/immersing-himself-bohemian-life)).
The same account describes two libel suits Gaeth brought and won during his presidency — one after
Catholic clergy accused missionaries of immorality and polygamy, and a second after a Prague Catholic
newspaper alleged the missionaries were German spies, which resulted in a retraction and damages awarded
to charity; no independent contemporary press source has been found to corroborate these suits (see
"What this profile does not claim," below). On 21 March 1931 Gaeth married Martha Theokla Kralicek,
briefly returning to the United States for the wedding before resuming his mission service with her as
his companion
([Church History Biographical Database](https://history.churchofjesuschrist.org/chd/individual/arthur-gaeth-1905?lang=eng)).
He led the mission until his release on 11 June 1936 and departure from the field on 20 July 1936, by
which point 128 people had joined the Church under its direction — a number that stopped growing once
Nazi occupation began
([Ensign Peak Foundation](https://ensignpeakfoundation.org/czechoslovak-mission-monument/)). One of the
missionaries who served under him, Wallace F. Toronto — who would return in 1936 to succeed Gaeth as
mission president — later recalled that Gaeth had "quite a journalistic disposition and a booming voice,"
and remembered Gaeth weeping during Toronto's own farewell address as a departing missionary
([Religious Studies Center, BYU](https://rsc.byu.edu/mission-president-spy/immersing-himself-bohemian-life)).
In April 1950 Gaeth attended a Czechoslovak Mission reunion in Salt Lake City alongside fellow former
mission leaders John A. Widtsoe and Ezra Taft Benson
([Religious Studies Center, BYU](https://rsc.byu.edu/mission-president-spy/satans-workers)).

## From mission president to war correspondent

After the Second World War, Gaeth returned to Europe as a journalist covering post-war diplomacy. He
reported live from Nuremberg, Germany for the Mutual Broadcasting System in a segment the U.S. National
Archives catalogs as "The Executions at Nuremberg": Gaeth described his earlier tour of the city prison
and witnessing the hanging of convicted Nazi war criminals, recounting the final words of Julius
Streicher ("The Bolshevists will one day hang you!"), Fritz Sauckel ("I die innocently."), and Arthur
Seyss-Inquart ("I hope that this execution is the last act of tragedy of the Second World War"), and
concluding that "there were no cowards among these men"
([National Archives and Records Administration](https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/voices-of-postwar.html)).
The National Archives catalogs the 23-minute recording, item 200-234, as dated 10 October 1946 — six days
before the Nuremberg executions are otherwise widely documented, including by the National WWII Museum,
as having taken place on 16 October 1946
([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/the-nuremberg-trial-and-its-legacy));
this profile reports that discrepancy rather than resolving it. A recording of the broadcast is held by
Stanford University's Hoover Institution under accession number XX625; the Hoover Institution's own
digital-collections record, Stanford University Libraries' SearchWorks catalog entry, and the Online
Archive of California's finding aid all describe this same single item, sharing the same accession number
and physical description, rather than three separate archival holdings
([Hoover Institution](https://digitalcollections.hoover.org/objects/124/arthur-gaeth-radio-news-broadcast);
[Stanford SearchWorks](https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/4086580);
[Online Archive of California](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/kt4w1033kj)). His personal papers,
spanning 1923 to 1983 — news reports, newspaper clippings, draft articles and photographs — are held at
Brigham Young University's L. Tom Perry Special Collections
([Brigham Young University Special Collections](https://archives.lib.byu.edu/repositories/ltpsc/resources/upb_mss1934)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not assert a fully independent birth year: Brigham Young University's own finding aid
for his papers gives two different years (1905 and 1915), and while the Church History Biographical
Database gives an exact birth date of 6 May 1905 — consistent with the separate, non-church account of him
being 24 years old at his 1929 mission call — that database is itself a church-affiliated source, not a
fully independent one. It does not resolve a six-day discrepancy between the National Archives' cataloged
date for the Nuremberg broadcast (10 October 1946) and the widely documented 16 October 1946 date of the
Nuremberg executions the broadcast describes. It does not treat the account of Gaeth's libel suits against
Catholic clergy and a Prague newspaper, or the story of his mother's patriarchal blessing, as independently
verified: both rest on a single Brigham Young University Religious Studies Center scholarly account, and no
outside contemporary press source has been found to corroborate the lawsuits' dates or outcomes. Finally,
it treats the Hoover Institution, Stanford SearchWorks, and Online Archive of California records for the
1946 broadcast as three catalog entries for one physical item, not three independent archival holdings.
