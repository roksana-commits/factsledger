## Overview

Elon Reeve Musk was born June 28, 1971, in Pretoria, South Africa, and left the country in 1988 before earning degrees in physics and economics from the University of Pennsylvania in 1997; he briefly enrolled in a Stanford physics PhD program but left after two days ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Elon-Musk)). He is best known as chief executive of the electric vehicle maker Tesla, which he joined as a major investor and became CEO of in 2004; as founder and CEO of the rocket company SpaceX, which he started in 2002; and as owner of the social media platform X, which he acquired as Twitter in October 2022 and rebranded in July 2023 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Elon-Musk)). This profile does not independently research those companies or Musk's other ventures in depth; it focuses on his documented relationship with OpenAI, the artificial intelligence company he co-founded and later sued.

## Co-founding OpenAI (2015)

In December 2015, Musk joined a group of Silicon Valley figures — including Sam Altman, Peter Thiel, Jessica Livingston, and Reid Hoffman — in launching OpenAI as a nonprofit artificial intelligence research lab, with the group pledging $1 billion toward the project ([CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2015/12/12/technology/openai-elon-musk/)). Musk and Altman served as co-chairs of the new organization ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/ericmack/2015/12/11/elon-musk-sam-altman-peter-thiel-others-launch-open-a-i-with-1-billion-donation/)). OpenAI's stated mission was to advance AI safely and make its research broadly available rather than let the technology be developed and controlled by a single company; Musk, who had previously voiced concern about AI's trajectory, said he wanted AI to function as "an extension of individual human wills" rather than a concentration of power in few hands ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/ericmack/2015/12/11/elon-musk-sam-altman-peter-thiel-others-launch-open-a-i-with-1-billion-donation/); [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2015/12/12/technology/openai-elon-musk/)).

## Departure from OpenAI's board (2018)

Musk resigned from OpenAI's board in late February 2018. OpenAI's public statement at the time said the move was meant "to eliminate a potential future conflict" as Tesla's own work increasingly focused on AI, and that Musk would continue to advise and donate to the nonprofit ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/02/21/elon-musk-is-leaving-the-board-of-openai.html)).

Accounts of what actually drove the departure differ. OpenAI's own later public statement — published in 2024 in response to Musk's lawsuit — says Musk instead sought majority equity, initial board control, and the CEO role for himself in early 2018, and had proposed merging OpenAI into Tesla, forwarding an internal email arguing OpenAI should "attach to Tesla as its cash cow." The same OpenAI statement says that when this was rejected, Musk told the team their "probability of success was 0," that he intended to pursue AGI within Tesla instead, and that OpenAI had received less than $45 million in funding from Musk against more than $90 million from other early donors ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-elon-musk/)). Because this account comes from OpenAI itself, published after it had already been sued by Musk, it is presented here as OpenAI's characterization rather than adopted as settled fact.

Independent reporting from before the lawsuit gives a broadly similar picture of internal conflict. Semafor reported in March 2023 that the publicly cited "conflict of interest" explanation was met with skepticism inside OpenAI at the time, that Musk had sought to take control of the organization after concluding by early 2018 that it had fallen behind Google, that co-founder Greg Brockman and others opposed the takeover attempt, and that Musk subsequently withheld donations he had previously planned to make ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/03/24/2023/the-secret-history-of-elon-musk-sam-altman-and-openai)).

## The lawsuit against OpenAI, Altman, and Microsoft

### Filing and refiling

Musk first sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in San Francisco Superior Court in February 2024, alleging the company had abandoned its founding nonprofit mission and become what the complaint called a "closed-source de facto subsidiary" of Microsoft, oriented toward "maximizing profits for Microsoft" rather than developing artificial general intelligence for humanity's benefit; the suit sought to compel OpenAI back toward its original mission ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/elon-musk-sues-openai-ceo-sam-altman-contract-breach-rcna141320)). On June 11, 2024, Musk's attorneys withdrew that suit without prejudice and without stating a reason, shortly before a judge was due to rule on OpenAI's motion to dismiss it ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/amp/news/money/2024/06/12/elon-musk-withdraws-lawsuit-against-openai/139309)).

In August 2024, Musk refiled in U.S. federal court in the Northern District of California, adding new claims including allegations of racketeering violations; his attorney Marc Toberoff called the new complaint "much stronger than the previous one." PhoneArena's report on that filing states it also named Greg Brockman as a defendant, but TechCrunch's independent account of the subsequent November 2024 amended complaint lists its defendants as OpenAI, Microsoft, Reid Hoffman, Dee Templeton, and California Attorney General Rob Bonta, without including Brockman — this profile does not resolve that discrepancy (see "What this profile does not claim") ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/musk-openai-new-lawsuit_id161151); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/14/musks-amended-lawsuit-against-openai-names-microsoft-as-defendant)). Brockman himself testified as a witness at the eventual trial. Microsoft was a defendant in the case by the time it reached summary judgment and trial, though sources read for this profile do not agree on exactly when Microsoft was added.

### Pretrial rulings and trial

The case proceeded in federal court as *Musk v. Altman*, No. 4:24-cv-4722-YGR, before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. On January 15, 2026, the court denied OpenAI's motion for summary judgment on claims of breach of charitable trust, constructive fraud, fraud, and unjust enrichment, and denied in part Microsoft's motion as well, sending those claims to trial ([U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, via FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-n-d-cal/118202562.html)).

A roughly three-week jury trial began in federal court in Oakland, California in April 2026. Witnesses included Musk, Altman, Brockman, Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella, and former OpenAI board members Helen Toner and Tasha McCauley ([PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/jury-sides-with-openai-saying-elon-musks-lawsuit-was-not-filed-on-time)). At trial, Musk pursued claims of breach of charitable trust and unjust enrichment against OpenAI and Altman, alleging Altman and Brockman had abandoned a shared commitment to keep OpenAI a nonprofit, while alleging Microsoft had aided and abetted that breach; Musk sought up to $150 billion in compensatory and punitive damages, along with the removal of Altman and Brockman from OpenAI's leadership ([Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/openai-not-liable-for-breach-of-charitable-trust-in-musk-altman-feud/); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/conormurray/2026/05/18/musk-to-appeal-openai-verdict-lawyer-says-war-is-not-over/)).

### The May 18, 2026 verdict

On May 18, 2026, a nine-member jury unanimously dismissed all of Musk's claims against OpenAI, Altman, and Microsoft after deliberating for less than two hours. The jury found that Musk had waited too long to sue: California's statute of limitations for breach of charitable trust runs three to four years, and the jury concluded Musk knew of the actions he complained of well before that window — measured from his 2018 board departure and a final financial contribution in 2020 — yet did not file suit until 2024 ([Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/openai-not-liable-for-breach-of-charitable-trust-in-musk-altman-feud/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/18/nx-s1-5822366/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed)). The jury's finding was on timeliness only; it did not rule on whether a breach of OpenAI's founding agreement had actually occurred. Judge Gonzalez Rogers accepted the verdict and dismissed the case, saying, "I've always said I would accept the jury's verdict. I think there's a substantial amount of evidence to support the jury's finding" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/18/nx-s1-5822366/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed)).

Musk's attorney Marc Toberoff said afterward that the response was "appeal," and, at a press conference, that the broader fight "is not over" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/18/nx-s1-5822366/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/conormurray/2026/05/18/musk-to-appeal-openai-verdict-lawyer-says-war-is-not-over/)). A separate attorney on the case, Steven Molo, was quoted the same day saying he "reserves the right to appeal," specifically contesting the statute-of-limitations finding ([Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/openai-not-liable-for-breach-of-charitable-trust-in-musk-altman-feud/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not adjudicate whether Musk's allegations against OpenAI, Altman, and Brockman were true — the jury ruled only that the claims were filed too late, and expressly did not decide whether a breach of OpenAI's founding agreement occurred. It does not adopt OpenAI's own account of why Musk left the board in 2018 as established fact; that account is presented as OpenAI's characterization, made after Musk sued the company, and is distinguished from the independent (though partially corroborating) reporting available from before the lawsuit. It does not state exactly when Microsoft was added as a defendant to the federal case, since the sources read here disagree on whether it was named in the original August 2024 federal complaint. It does not assert that Greg Brockman was formally a named defendant, since sources conflict on that point; it does state that he is documented as a trial witness. It does not confirm whether Musk's stated intent to appeal the May 2026 verdict was ever converted into an actual notice of appeal, or that case's status as of this profile's last update. It does not cover Musk's biography, other companies, wealth, or personal life beyond the brief overview needed for context, and it does not link to any profile of OpenAI's 2026 Navier–Stokes claim, since no source read here shows a direct connection between Musk and that dispute.
