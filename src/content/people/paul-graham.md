## Overview

Paul Graham is an American computer scientist, essayist and investor. He holds a PhD in computer science from Harvard University, where he originally intended to concentrate on artificial intelligence, and later studied painting at the Accademia delle Belle Arti in Florence and the Rhode Island School of Design ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/global/2010/1108/companies-y-combinator-paul-graham-disruptor.html)). Graham's own biography additionally states an AB from Cornell University, though no independently fetched source read for this profile separately confirms that degree ([paulgraham.com](https://www.paulgraham.com/bio.html); see "What this profile does not claim"). He is best known for co-founding the web-store company Viaweb, for his essays published at paulgraham.com, and for co-founding and leading the startup accelerator [Y Combinator](/organizations/y-combinator/).

## Viaweb and early career

In 1995, Graham co-founded Viaweb with Robert Morris and others; Forbes describes it as the first software-as-a-service company, providing software that let merchants build online storefronts ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/global/2010/1108/companies-y-combinator-paul-graham-disruptor.html)). Yahoo acquired Viaweb in 1998 for roughly $50 million, after which it became Yahoo Store ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/global/2010/1108/companies-y-combinator-paul-graham-disruptor.html)).

## Essays and Hacker News

Graham began publishing essays at paulgraham.com in 2001 ([paulgraham.com](https://www.paulgraham.com/bio.html)). Forbes describes one early essay, "Beating the Averages" — which praised the Lisp programming language — as drawing around 50,000 page views, and describes another, "How to Start a Startup," based on a talk Graham gave at Harvard, as shaping the thinking about angel investing that led him toward founding Y Combinator ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/global/2010/1108/companies-y-combinator-paul-graham-disruptor.html)).

In February 2007, Graham built a site called Startup News, originally as a demonstration of Arc, a programming language he was developing; he said at the time he built it to recreate the startup-focused community that had once existed on Reddit before Reddit's user base broadened, quoting himself as saying "Reddit used to have a good concentration of startup-related links, but that's because so many of Reddit's initial users were connected in some way to Y Combinator" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/05/18/the-evolution-of-hacker-news/)). Startup News was later renamed Hacker News and grew into a community site for programmers and founders ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/05/18/the-evolution-of-hacker-news/)). Graham ran Hacker News through Y Combinator until March 29, 2014, when he handed day-to-day control to a YC team — Kat Manalac, Garry Tan, Daniel Gackle, Kevin Hale and Nick Sivo — saying he would remain "around as a user," though less frequently than when he felt he had to check the site every hour or so ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=980463)).

## Y Combinator

In 2005, Graham co-founded the startup accelerator Y Combinator together with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris and Trevor Blackwell, starting it as a summer program near Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/global/2010/1108/companies-y-combinator-paul-graham-disruptor.html)). Y Combinator's own account credits the same four co-founders and says it has since funded more than 3,000 startups, including Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe and Reddit ([paulgraham.com](https://www.paulgraham.com/bio.html)). Additional detail on Y Combinator's history, business model and later leadership is covered in its own profile.

Graham led Y Combinator as its president for its first nine years. In February 2014, he announced he was stepping down, to be succeeded by [Sam Altman](/people/sam-altman/), who had been a part-time YC partner since 2011. Graham explained the decision in a phone interview: "Really, what's going on is that YC needs to get bigger, and I am not really much of a manager. Sam Altman is." He said he intended to keep holding office hours with startups and to return to writing essays, "maybe about topics other than startups" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/21/sam-altman-taking-over-as-president-of-y-combinator-replacing-paul-graham-at-the-helm)).

### The 2019 Altman departure: two accounts

Sam Altman left Y Combinator in 2019, the same year OpenAI announced a for-profit subsidiary with Altman as its chief executive. Accounts of the circumstances of that departure differ, and this profile states both rather than adopting either.

On May 30, 2024, Graham posted on X disputing reports that Y Combinator had "fired" Altman. As reported by TechCrunch, he wrote: "People have been claiming [Y Combinator] fired Sam Altman. That's not true," and said that when OpenAI announced its for-profit subsidiary in 2019, Jessica Livingston told Altman he needed to choose between the two roles: "if he was going to work full-time on OpenAI, we should find someone else to run YC, and he agreed" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/30/paul-graham-claims-altman-wasnt-fired-from-y-combinator)). Forbes reported the same posts, quoting Graham as saying "We didn't want him to leave, just to choose one or the other" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/roberthart/2024/05/30/sam-altman-wasnt-fired-from-y-combinator-founder-says-as-openai-chief-faces-criticism-from-ex-board-member/)).

Both outlets reported that this account contradicts earlier reporting. TechCrunch said The Wall Street Journal and The Washington Post had previously reported that Altman was forced to resign after YC partners raised concerns he was prioritizing his own projects over his YC duties, and cited one account that Graham cut short an overseas trip to remove Altman in person ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/30/paul-graham-claims-altman-wasnt-fired-from-y-combinator)). Forbes similarly reported that The Washington Post, citing three sources, said Graham removed Altman over concerns about divided loyalties, and quoted former OpenAI board member Helen Toner telling the TED AI Show that the real reasons for the departure had been "hushed up at the time" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/roberthart/2024/05/30/sam-altman-wasnt-fired-from-y-combinator-founder-says-as-openai-chief-faces-criticism-from-ex-board-member/)).
