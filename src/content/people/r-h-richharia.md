## Overview

R. H. Richharia was a Cambridge-trained rice geneticist who directed India's Central Rice Research
Institute (CRRI) at Cuttack, Odisha, from 1959 to 1966, and whose research on clonal propagation of rice
seedlings — showing yield increases of 17% to 61% over conventional transplanting — was published in the
journal *Nature* in May 1962
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture)).
Born in 1909 in Nandanvara village, Hoshangabad district, Madhya Pradesh, he held an M.Sc. in Botany from
Nagpur and completed a Ph.D. at Cambridge University under Professor P. S. Hudson
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture);
[Countercurrents](https://countercurrents.org/2021/02/top-rice-scientist-dr-richharia-who-sacrificed-much-to-protect-interests-of-farmers/)).

## Removal from CRRI and the Green Revolution dispute

Richharia publicly opposed the arbitrary introduction of Green Revolution high-yield seed varieties,
arguing instead for rice development built on improved indigenous varieties, which he warned were less
vulnerable to disease
([Countercurrents](https://countercurrents.org/2021/02/top-rice-scientist-dr-richharia-who-sacrificed-much-to-protect-interests-of-farmers/)).
In 1966 he was removed from the CRRI directorship; the water and electricity supply to his official
residence was disconnected, and he left for Bhopal
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture)).
He filed a case in the Odisha High Court against his removal, and after winning it, returned to CRRI to
find his official office locked and his research papers taken by the authorities
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture)).

## Indigenous rice conservation

From 1971 to 1976, working across what was then undivided Madhya Pradesh, Richharia collected and
catalogued around 17,000 indigenous rice cultivars
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture);
[Countercurrents](https://countercurrents.org/2021/02/top-rice-scientist-dr-richharia-who-sacrificed-much-to-protect-interests-of-farmers/)).
He went on to head the Madhya Pradesh Rice Research Institute and to serve as an agricultural adviser to
the state government, and later continued cataloguing and documenting traditional rice varieties from his
own residence and farm near Bhopal until his death in 1996
([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/agriculture/r-h-richharia-is-the-unsung-hero-of-indian-agriculture);
[Countercurrents](https://countercurrents.org/2021/02/top-rice-scientist-dr-richharia-who-sacrificed-much-to-protect-interests-of-farmers/)).
A separate account reports that his health deteriorated after the 1984 Bhopal gas disaster
([Countercurrents](https://countercurrents.org/2025/06/this-plan-of-a-top-scientist-can-help-to-greatly-improve-rice-cultivation-while-avoiding-high-risk-gm-ge-technology/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not resolve conflicting accounts of exactly when Richharia's later posts began and
ended, or the precise scale of his final cataloguing work — sources consulted give different end-years
for his time leading the Madhya Pradesh Rice Research Institute (1976 versus 1978) and different figures
for a later "rice encyclopaedia" (2,000 varieties in one account, 19,000 cultivars in another), which
appear to describe different things rather than a single reconcilable number. It also does not assert a
precise end-date for his CRRI directorship beyond noting that one source gives 1966 and another gives
1967.
