## Overview

Lorenzo Nigro is an Italian archaeologist specialising in the archaeology of the Near East and the Phoenician-Punic
world. He graduated cum laude in Archaeology and History of Art of the Ancient Near East from Sapienza University
of Rome in 1992 and completed his PhD there in 1997
([Sapienza University of Rome](https://phd.uniroma1.it/web/LORENZO-NIGRO_nC205_EN.aspx)). From 1998 to 2005 he
served as Curator of the Gregorian Egyptian Museum at the Vatican Museums, before returning to Sapienza, where he
became Associate Professor in 2005 and was promoted to Full Professor in the Department of Classics in 2021
([Sapienza University of Rome](https://phd.uniroma1.it/web/LORENZO-NIGRO_nC205_EN.aspx)).

Since 2002 he has directed the Sapienza archaeological mission at Motya, the ancient Phoenician city on an island
off the west coast of Sicily, and since 2005 he has directed the Sapienza expedition to Tell es-Sultan, the site of
ancient Jericho in the West Bank
([Sapienza University of Rome](https://phd.uniroma1.it/web/LORENZO-NIGRO_nC205_EN.aspx)). He has also directed
excavations at Khirbet al-Batrawy, an Early Bronze Age fortified city roughly 25 kilometres north of Amman, Jordan
([Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/excavation-khirbet-al-batrawy-gives-clues-ancient-urban-centre)).

## The Motya "sacred pool" finding

In a 2022 paper in the journal Antiquity, Nigro proposed a reinterpretation of a large rectangular basin at Motya
long identified as an artificial harbor, or "kothon." He argued instead that the structure was a sacred freshwater
pool at the centre of a monumental circular sanctuary housing three temples, fed by underground springs and never
connected to the sea during the Phoenician city's occupation between the 8th and 5th centuries BC
([Antiquity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/sacred-pool-of-baal-a-reinterpretation-of-the-kothon-at-motya/329646E6561765FD30A9D6EC5FD5B6CB)).
The finding drew international press coverage. Science News reported that the pool, built between roughly 550 and
520 BC, once held a statue of the god Ba'al and appeared to align with specific stars and constellations on
solstices, suggesting a use in astronomical observation as well as worship
([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/sacred-pool-ancient-seafarers-phoenicians-largest-mediterranean)).
Smithsonian Magazine quoted Nigro telling The Guardian that "the only good mirror in antiquity is water," in
reference to the pool's likely use for observing star positions reflected on its surface
([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/ancient-harbor-sacred-pool-scanning-stars-180979997/)).

## Jordan: Khirbet al-Batrawy

Discussing his excavation of Khirbet al-Batrawy with the Jordan Times in 2017, Nigro described a fortified hilltop
settlement, established around 3000 BC, with a four-line defensive wall system, a palace, and evidence of
long-distance trade reaching Egypt: "People living in rural villages along the banks of the Zarqa River... chose a
hilltop site as the seat of a communal temple and the palace of a ruling institution, which organised long-distance
trade [up to Egypt as finds demonstrated]"
([Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/excavation-khirbet-al-batrawy-gives-clues-ancient-urban-centre)).

## Recognition

In 2019, the arts publication Finestre sull'Arte reported that Nigro won the Silvia Dell'Orso Prize for his book
"Jericho. The Prehistoric Revolution" (Edizioni Il Vomere), selected by the prize's scientific committee from 18
nominations submitted from across Italy
([Finestre sull'Arte](https://www.finestresullarte.info/en/news/archaeologist-lorenzo-nigro-wins-the-silvia-dell-orso-prize-2019)).
